Elias and Lucas met while studying at UdK Berlin, where they began working together on university projects. Together, they co-founded Studio HanLi and HAL Typefaces, working across graphic design, typography, and typefaces.

We talked to them on one of the warmest days of summer about their long partnership, life in the studio, success (and the resistance to it), and the importance of taking care of yourself to maintain the practice. Watching the recording again, we cackled every time Lucas tried to insert the word "collaboration" into one of his answers.

I really enjoy sort of crate digging, doing visual research on old specimens and historic sources. It's a big joy. I like buying old antiquarian books online and stuff, or going to certain libraries. It's a method of form finding that we quite like.

We don't really make revivals at HAL Type, but it's part of the process and part of the development to have a point of departure and say, "Oh, here's an interesting idea that maybe was forgotten at some stage in type design history." And there's certain aspects we can maybe incorporate into an updated version or updated design. And sometimes it's staying closer to maybe the source material, and sometimes it's moving away really far from the source material. But it's a nice starting point where we review a lot of this stuff that we find and say, this is interesting, or this could lead to something else, and then it becomes part of the story.

We just launched a new website, and we really like to share the story and development behind every typeface. For each typeface release, we write a release note, include some historical material, and try to show, "Ah, look, we were looking at this, and it maybe led to some new ideas." It's kind of nice to give credit to old masters or old designs. I think it also makes it more interesting for type users because they understand where the typeface comes from, what it means, and maybe the stylistic choices that went into it.

I think it's quite interesting for type users when they have more of a relationship with the typefaces they're using, instead of just buying a typeface on MyFonts and having no idea who designs it, what it means, or where it comes from. So I feel like that story also makes it more fun for graphic designers. Did that answer your question? I'm trying to summarize how we come up with ideas for typefaces. Obviously, they take years to make, and there might be people helping us or jumping in to do certain things. We also extend languages sometimes, and then we have people who can read and write Cyrillic because we can't. There are also aspects where people jump in and help. But the conception not always, but often, is kind of rooted in historical sources or ideas.
