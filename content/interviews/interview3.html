Fabian Harb is a Swiss graphic and typeface designer – we reached him from Bremen while he was in Portugal. Together with Johannes Breyer, he directs the digital type foundry Dinamo. Fabian was our first interviewee, who helped us to get rid of our anxiety about interviews – even when connection issues meant we had to do the conversation without cameras. He gave us insights into the behind-the-scenes at Dinamo, how he and Johannes started designing typefaces together and how that developed in opening a foundry and running it together with a team of different specialists and often across many places!

Mira

Maybe you can also introduce yourself. What are you doing? What are you working on? How is Portugal?

Fabian

So, I'm Fabian. I'm part of Dinamo. I started Dinamo together with Johannes many, many years ago. It started very small and slow, but by now it grew into a quite large thing, involving many different people: Typedesigners, Engineers, Communication, Support, Hardware, Graphic Design, Website development, Administration, even Legal things sometimes. We didn't study type design, Johannes and I. But from an interest in book design and editorial design, we developed an interest in typefaces. And from being interested in typefaces, we started to search and use special versions of fonts, and finally went into starting to design our own ones.

Back then, the programs and tools to design typefaces were much more difficult to get and to learn. So, we started to exchange our knowledge, helping each other with the problems that we had and the things that we did not know. Very naturally we started to also design typefaces together and around 2014 or 2015 we had the first typefaces that felt finished. Friends started to also ask us “Hey, can we use your fonts?” and slowly we started to do small customizations or special typefaces for other people, or sometimes even for companies.

Then, around 2016 or 2017, we won a Swiss Design Award. Which is a super special kind of award, coming with a sort of “stipendium”. It comes with 25,000 Swiss Francs, a bit more then 25,000 Euros that you can use unconditionally. It's not linked to a project, but more just a boost that you can take for whatever you need in this moment of your career. So, that was the moment when Johannes and I basically said “hey, let's fully focus on designing typefaces. Let's not take on more graphic design projects for as long as this money lasts.” From that money we then managed to focus only on designing fonts for the next months, and finish and extend quite a few of our early families. And once they were finished we slowly started to put them online and license them publicly as well.

So, quite naturally Johannes and I both stopped doing graphic design individually, but only design and publish typefaces together. Like that, Dinamo became the main activity of the two of us. Over time we had interns and then some interns became frequent collaborators when projects were bigger and allowed for more people to work on. At some point the first intern started to stay and became our first employee. It was all very slow and organic. And over the time, it really became a company with a shop and more and more typefaces. Most of them first for our library, and then later we started to make custom versions of them for clients. Or sometimes its even possible to design a new font from scratch for somebody else. And alongside of this, the team also grew according to Dinamo’s needs and interests. And today we are around 20 people – some of them part-time and some of them full-time, that run and shape Dinamo with all its different outputs.

Mira

My question would be directly about founding Dinamo. Now we know the reasons, where it came from. But you also had to decide together with Johannes “Okay, are we doing this? Are we doing this really together?” I guess some questions like this developed … How did you find each other? Were you already friends or working together, studying together – because it's like a huge decision to...

Fabian

Yeah, totally. I think it was a lot of luck. I always think even if a lot of things click, it's so complex to work together. So many things around you somehow have to fall in place? Johannes and I were first introduced by Larissa Kasper from Kasper-Florio, who is a really old friend of mine. We both come from the same small town and know each other quite a bit longer than being graphic designers. Sankt Gallen where we both come from is such a small town that you cannot study graphic design there, you have to go somewhere else. The both of us did entrance examinations together, but then decided to go to different schools. Larissa studied in Zurich while I went to Basel. But Johannes, who is from Germany, from Erlangen, he moved to Zurich for his studies and then happened to be in the same class with Larissa. So she was the one that introduced us to each other and said "Hey, you should, you should meet, you will get along."

During the years that we studied, we often visited each other in Basel and Zurich. Or went to see talks and shows together. But then in 2012, I did an internship in Amsterdam while Johannes went there for an exchange semester at the Rietveld at the same time. So, during that time and both being in a new place together, we started to hang out much more. And Amsterdam has this amazing library specialized in book design and graphic design and type design. They have an amazing collection of estates of Dutch designers. And since we both grew an interest in typedesign at the same time, we went there many times to look at stuff. And spending time together and sharing interests, we very naturally started helping each other on some typeface projects as well.

A bit later, I think in 2013, we were invited by Radim Pesko, by one of the teachers that Johannes had at the Rietveld, to work on a publication for the Biennale in Brno in the Czech Republic. Like that we got to work on the catalog for the exhibition. But actually because the content came in very late, we mostly used the time to work on a special typeface for the catalogue. That was really the “click", the framework in which we started to work together quite closely. It worked really well and we enjoyed it a lot. Of course we weren’t able to directly start working on everything together full time. But I would say, that was the foundation for Dinamo to continue to work together? So, in those years we still worked individually and on graphic design commissions mostly – but whenever we had free time we would keep collaborating on font projects.

After Amsterdam, both Johannes and I moved to Berlin. Johannes direct went freelance on his own. But me, at first I still worked for studio 4 days a week. And only in 2014, when me and Nina had our first son, we moved back to Switzerland and there I changed to working independently as well. One day a week I was able to teach at a design school, which gave me great stability. But for the rest of the week I worked on graphic design commissions and together with Johannes on typefaces in the free time. And then, as I said, in 2017 I think, it was when we won the design award. That was really the moment when we said let's fully focus on the typefaces, let's fully focus on Dinamo.

So, most of Dinamo really happened super organically. But, of course, there were always difficulties and challenges to figure out how to work together – especially also since Johannes and I never had a studio together? Up until today actually. On the upside, I think that our setup was really allowing us to find "our” solutions and the perfect overlap of our interests. But on the flipside of course it also required a lot of patience and discussion and testing. Not only within Dinamo and “how to run a company”, but for me as well privately and with a baby, with a family.

Something I wonder if we had an actually studio together, and would have collaborated in the same place: A lot of conversations might have been easier – but it probably would also have created more challenges to unify it with my private life. Probably with the distance, there was automatically more flexibility needed. And the differences between Johannes and me also didnt show and weigh so much?

Today, with our main studio and core team sitting in Berlin with Johannes, that changed a bit of course. But for me being here in Porto and a big part of our type design team spread out between different places, we still have that as integral part of Dinamo: Mike and Roberto work from Italy, Jovana and Ruggero are working from Belgrade with us. We have Fabiola, a collaborator that is based in Costa Rica. Samira is based in Switzerland, Olga in Moscow, Arnaud in Paris. And then we closely collaborate with a additional freelancers on multi script projects as well: Mingoo designing Hangul with us from Seoul, Korea. Wael and KJ designed quite some Arabics with us already. Panos is doing most of our Greeks from Athens, or Boom who designs Thai is working from The Hague, for example.

Would you say it’s decentralized?

Fabian

In type design, collaborating on projects from different places is really possible and actually often nice for the projects: In comparison to graphic design the work takes much more time and is less hectic and spontaneous. So, things and tasks can be divided between a team quite naturally and everybody can work in their own ways and speeds. For example, you develop a design and then maybe somebody takes over and adds a mono space or extends the weight spectrum or other people help to add different writing systems, etc. Bringing together all these people in one place would be very difficult and maybe also not a 100% compatible. But of course you sometimes miss the social aspects of working in the same space together? There is always pros and cons of course. And there is always changes and new challenges as well …

Running a company and working together with a lot of people, you realize and learn so many of new things constantly. The structural framework around the work is super important and, in a way, also interesting. Many times it is a bit scary at first, but it also brings huge opportunities to find out how your setup makes the most sense for you. It's not always easy, but I think super good, super helpful to work through this all together.

Phuong

I actually have another question, because you mentioned that a part of your team is so spread out. Is there a special way or a special mode of working that you have to adapt to make it work efficiently? Because you say it's not always working, right?

Fabian

Totally. On a practical level, I think it is helping a lot that it was always like this and that its clear and fine for everybody in the team that our work setups are ever-changing and that its part of our work to question, and to react and adjust how we work together constantly. But on a personal level, and especially for me being away from Berlin and the biggest part of our team, sometimes I am a bit jealous of the social aspects and qualities that they have, working together in one space. The time together over lunch, or after work, when other topics and inputs come up. Or also just the spontaneous moments, ideating or even misunderstanding and coming to unexpected, accidental and surprising results. Those kind of things are much more touch to provoke on distance.

Or if you can be together in one place, decision making is much more efficient. If you really sit down, take your time, you discuss through it, and you call the shots. And on a distance you much more quickly start a conversation as a chat, but then the other person has something else on their mind, and it's kind of lingering.

Then again for type design it can be great to ping pong: Start an idea and then pass it on to somebody else. And then give that person some time to come back with it after they added their ideas. If that happens in the same place you much more quickly check back to see what everybody else thinks. While on distance you tend to visualise and develop ideas more to a point that is understandable without much mediation?

But in the end, for all kind of work, I think the most important thing is the communication around it: Since all we do is team work, getting everybody on board, making sure that everybody has all of the needed informations, can bring in their ideas, and so on. Obviously this takes a lot of time and energy, and is not always easy. But it is so important and it makes such a difference when everybody is connected and talking with each other. Both the process and the result.

Mira

This also fits to one question we thought about. It was about which role does good communication have in effective collaboration at Dinamo? Like every one of us knows, communication is key – but we also realize that more and more in our collaborative working, especially also with language barriers. So which role has communication at Dinamo and also maybe you have tools or how do you do it on a daily basis? This thought also connected to the note on how you give critiques or feedback to each other.

Fabian

I am mixing a bit between different kinds of communications and conversations here – but I fully agree that having all of them is equally important. To keep everybody in the look and connected, we do different kinds of “team calls” and meetings: In the studio in Berlin they sit together every Monday morning to quickly sync about the weekend and what everybody did, and also the week ahead and what everybody is going to be busy with. And once a month the rest of us dial in to join them remotely, which is always super nice. Additionally we also have a big Engineering and Type Design meeting every month where we show each other a little bit what we are doing: Designs and tools and multi script projects and client commissions and so on. Then zoomed in for typeface developments, we usually have a lot of smaller check ins to look at the designs and feedback each other. This we mostly do via PDF proof documents in which we can easily collect everybody's thoughts together in one place. Then for bigger Dinamo topics we usually do online calls as well, and discuss things like website updates or newsletters or other "brand” and communication work. And then there is also campaigns that we design to launch a new typeface design. This often involves external graphic designers or illustrators or animators or so on, and therefore calls. And lastly we also try to meet in real life as much as we can, on the occasion of giving a lecture, teaching a workshop, or attending a conference or so. Especially for really tough and personal talking points, there is nothing like spending time together to figure things out.

Phuong

Also since the team is so spread out and you have a lot of collaborators, how are decisions made at Dinamo? Because I can imagine a lot of times decision making involves a compromise and maybe no one is happy with the compromise?

Fabian

Very good question. I don't think there's one way how we always solve and decide things. I think it's very situation-specific, set-up-specific, who is involved, what is the project, and so on? For me personally, working together in a team, having different voices always feels more exciting always. I probably couldnt even do any of it alone? Of course there is stuff that I have clear personal preferences about – and sometimes they are different from the preference of other team members. But zoomed out, I find it most exciting to see where something becomes equally interesting to everybody involved. Kind of letting the process and the different perspectives guide you to a point where you say “this is what everybody involved finds exciting”. Of course you could call that “a compromise”. But in this moment I don't see a compromise as a bad thing or as non-quality. On the contrary, I think it's super nice because it is kind of the result and the consequence of these people, of this situation. And if you had a different situation, a different team, then you would also very naturally end up with something else. Especially if you do this over a long time, it stays exciting because you don't repeat yourself. Plus it feels way less influential what other people outside of the team think about it? Even if somebody else will hate it, it somehow does not matter much because we all in the team like it together?

Mira

Is there a difference in custom typefaces – that the clients influence more the decisions or are you more or less free in what you do and they just trust you? How is it usually?

Fabian

Of course, clients have quite a big say in what you do and what a project becomes. We sometimes have people or companies that come to us and give us a lot of freedom, that give us almost a carte blanche to design something for them. But most of the time they come some sort a “problem”: a technical requirement, a specific function the type should fulfil for them, and also often they have time or budget restrictions of course. But since you can start with a pre-existing typeface for a custom font and adjust it, usually there is a large range of possibilities and you can almost always find a way to make sense of a brief. If the timeline is short you can only do changes that do not require a lot of testing and development. If the budget is small, you can organise a customisation in phases and extend and update a design once they have more money at hand again, and so on? Sometimes these restrictions can be a bit of a challenge and you wish they would be different to reach the fullest potential of an idea. But most of the time we are quite good in finding something that is exciting and interesting for us to be doing, despite any odds. In that perspective, limitations can be quite helpful and inspiring as well?

Mira

They give a structure you can work in.

Fabian

Totally! I always love when a specific framework is leading you to something that you wouldn’t do in another framework. But of course you something find something exciting for yourselves but the client is not sharing the perspective. Sometimes that can feel a bit frustrating. Yet the nice thing with these kinds of ideas is that you can keep them in mind and often test or use them again later, on other projects. We had this a couple of times already, that a client did not like an idea and then we just did it anyways for our own library.

But it always takes times to get to know each other and find out of course. With more experience you definitely grow a better gut feeling. But it also happened already that we see the work of somebody and it resonates and feels close to your own interests, but when you meet the person you realise they work in very different ways from your own processes and that you wouldn't be compatible to really work together side by side and very closely? Because we already have a quite big and well tuned setup and team. So, everybody new needs to fit with all that is already there, right!

Completely. Sometimes you can get some insights if you know somebody that already worked with a potential collaborator previously. Especially if you have to find somebody swift, I always love this “word of mouth” recommendations or endorsements the most!

We definitely had both already. But luckily its mostly been positive surprises. Sometimes its even something that is also interesting to a project. Or sometimes its more just an already nice connection and exchange that gets even more exciting and interesting? But there has been some occasional hick-ups as well. Where you hoped for something and it just didnt happen that exact way or live up to your expectations fully? But we certainly learned to protect ourselves from that as well better. If you work with somebody new, its always nice to start with something small, and with a lot of communication around the project. And then you an also react and adjust if needed during the process still.

Fabian

Totally. Its always good to go slow and careful in the beginnings. In a way its the same for new collaborations and for new projects: Giving yourself time to fully find out and define. And only once you fully know you lock in and move forward. But of course this isnt always completely possible. Sometimes a bit of risk taking and gut feeling and trusting your intuition is unavoidable I guess.

Mega.

Totally. Very often I think that this way there is great quality control as well. When everybody who is involved in something is excited and happy with the result, I think it's already good enough. Phuong

On that note, what is the project that involves the most people in Dinamo?

Fabian

Everything Dinamo does involves quite a lot of people by now. If you design a typeface that goes to the library, that probably touches everybody that is part of Dinamo at some point in time. Some of us work on the idea for the design, more people get involved during the development and the the production, then there is engineering, kerning, mastering. Afterwards some of us are creating a campaign, to accompanying it when it gets launched. And if all goes full circle, maybe there will even be requests coming in inquiring for a custom versions of the design and then somebody creates an estimate and sets up a service agreement, and then and then the process starts anew again with the type designers adding tweaks to the typeface, before handing it over to the engineers who do the re-mastering and so on? But of course this all is happening over long time stretches. It's not everybody working together on one project at the same time – it's more phases.

But since over the years we had so many requests and opportunities to extend it to support Greek and Arabic and Thai and Georgian and Devanagari and so on, that it became one of our most meaningful Dinamo project to me because it gave us the opportunity to connect with so many great people and it bears so many memories of working together on it.

Mira

Trading work for work is always super nice, I agree. But it can also quickly become more complex once you enter a more commercial setup of course. With typefaces becoming products, there often is opportunities for nice partnerships. Like for example multiple designers contributing different writing systems to a bigger typeface family, and instead of charging for their work, they later receive a royalty split from the licensing sales of the design later on. That way you not only tap into competence that you wouldn’t have yourself, but you also create more stability for each other, as afterwards you might get a small percentage of every sale of the font. And not only if that very specific writing system sells that you are able to design? But again, this isnt equally easy for all writing systems of course: A well produced Latin typeface for example might contain 700 or 800 glyphs. While a Hangul typeface might require more than 10’000 glyphs before you can properly use it. So, designing and contributing a Latin design is much more quickly doable than designing and contributing a Hangul design for a collaborative project for example? In those moment I feel it really is important that there is clear monetary solutions in exchange for the work. At Dinamo we often agree to develop larger writing systems over longer time and pay the designers in instalments that we can more easily afford. Or sometimes we are lucky and we have a bigger client that needs a larger license and then they crossfinance the development through that.
